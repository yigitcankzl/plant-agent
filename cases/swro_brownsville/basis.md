# Brownsville seawater desalination: data sheet

Numbers are from the Lower Rio Grande Valley Brownsville Seawater Desalination Demonstration Project Feasibility Study (Dannenbaum Engineering and URS for the Brownsville Public Utilities Board, November 2004) (2004 PDF p.1). Citations are PDF page numbers of that study. The drawing is a PlantAgent sketch of the Section 4 flowsheet, not a copy of Figure 4-5 (2004 PDF p.182). Anything the study does not state is marked (ILLUSTRATIVE).

## Capacity

The concept is an initial 25 MGD finished-water plant that can be expanded to 100 MGD in 25 MGD increments (2004 PDF p.384). Five of six seawater RO trains produce the initial 25 MGD. The sixth train is the spare (2004 PDF p.195). Each seawater train is designed for 5 MGD of permeate (2004 PDF p.195).

## Source water

The intake is in side channels of the Brownsville Ship Channel (2004 PDF p.27). Ship-channel TDS in the reviewed record runs from about 11,700 ppm to about 49,000 ppm. The average near 36,100 ppm is why the study set aside the extreme single points, and the design range kept after that review is still 11,700 to 49,000 mg/L (2004 PDF p.112 and p.189). TSS is about 36 mg/L and has been as high as 153 mg/L (2004 PDF p.112). Temperature is typically 60 F to 95 F, annual average 75 F (2004 PDF p.192).

A full ion table comparable to a membrane projection is not printed in the sections cited here. ILLUSTRATIVE: do not fill missing ions from a standard seawater recipe and call it Brownsville data.

## Process

Intake screens are a copper-nickel alloy, sized to limit impingement and entrainment (2004 PDF p.186). Ballasted flocculation: three trains in parallel, each hydraulically rated for 21 MGD. Two trains carry the full finished-water capacity and the third is spare (2004 PDF p.186).

Steam from a co-located power plant preheats the RO feed. The study's rule of thumb is that high-pressure pump discharge pressure falls about 1 percent per degree Fahrenheit of feed heating. Heating the average 75 F water to 90 F is described as about a 15 percent pressure reduction (2004 PDF p.192). The heater is a 36 inch in-line steam heater on each feed stream (2004 PDF p.192).

Sulfuric acid may be used to hold about pH 6.5. If the seawater is already acidified ahead of ballasted flocculation, a further 20 mg/L of concentrated sulfuric acid is the study's figure to move pH from 7.0 to 6.0. Antiscalant is provided for even if piloting does not confirm the dose. The dose itself is not given (2004 PDF p.193). ILLUSTRATIVE: an antiscalant concentration in mg/L is not in the study.

Cartridge filters are the last barrier for solids larger than 5 microns (2004 PDF p.193).

Seawater RO: six parallel trains, 5 MGD permeate each, two stages, six-element pressure vessels, reject staging used to push overall recovery to about 60 percent. Brine is about 3.33 MGD per train, 16.67 MGD for the 25 MGD plant at that recovery (2004 PDF p.195). About 44 percent of the combined permeate goes to the partial second pass (2004 PDF p.195). First-pass TDS is described as falling from about 36,000 ppm to about 380 ppm (2004 PDF p.196).

Energy recovery selected in the screening is a Pelton wheel, one turbine per seawater train. The positive-displacement work exchanger was the other alternative and was not the preferred selection (2004 PDF p.145). Without energy recovery the study says each seawater high-pressure pump would be about 4,000 HP (2004 PDF p.143).

The conceptual-design pump for each seawater train is approximately 1,100 psig with a 2,900 HP variable-speed motor (2004 PDF p.197).
The mechanically coupled Pelton turbine contributes shaft work and is described as reducing electrical demand by about
33 percent (2004 PDF p.197). A motor check must therefore compare the motor with pump shaft demand net of the turbine,
not with the gross pump duty before recovered shaft work.

Second pass: two brackish trains, one of them spare. Each is rated for about 11.1 MGD of feed at the required pressure, with a 300 HP variable-speed drive. Recovery is about 90 percent. Concentrate is recycled ahead of the seawater trains so the composite recovery stays about 60 percent (2004 PDF p.195 and p.196).

Stabilization is intended to add about 40 mg/L of calcium. Both pebble lime and calcite filters were screened (2004 PDF p.144 and p.109). The study does not pick a finished-water boron limit in the pages used here.

Of the primary-treatment product, about 1.84 MGD (1,280 gpm) is identified as cooling-tower makeup for the co-located power plant, and about 0.16 MGD (110 gpm) as demineralized water (2004 PDF p.197).

Brine from the initial plant is 16.67 MGD at 60 percent recovery, as above. The diffuser appendix repeats the 25 MGD initial capacity, the 100 MGD build-out and the 60 percent recovery (2004 PDF p.384).

Power cost used for the heater example is 0.035 dollars per kWh (2004 PDF p.192). The comment responses in the front of the PDF say that tariff was already considered low and that the current municipal rate was higher; they do not replace it with a new number in the section cited (2004 PDF p.10).

## Not in the study

ILLUSTRATIVE if a model needs them: membrane area and flux (gfd is not stated in the sections above), element manufacturer, vessel count, intake-pump head, and a full ion recipe. The 2004 text says pilot testing would still be required for the acid dose and the antiscalant (2004 PDF p.193).

## Request

Can this 25 MGD concept hold about 60 percent overall recovery and the finished-water duty on the cold, salty corner of the ship-channel record (about 60 F and 49,000 mg/L TDS) with one seawater train out of service? What limits it first, and what is the cheapest way to stay at 25 MGD? Those corners are the study's own figures (2004 PDF p.112, p.192 and p.195).
